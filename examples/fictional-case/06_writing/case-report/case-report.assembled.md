# Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms after a urate-lowering drug in a patient with chronic kidney disease: a case report

Keywords: drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; allopurinol; HLA-B*58:01; chronic kidney disease; case report

\newpage

## Abstract

### Introduction

Allopurinol is a well-recognised trigger of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms
(DRESS), and the risk is higher when the starting dose is not adjusted for renal function and in
carriers of HLA-B*58:01 [1–4]. This report
illustrates how these risk factors combined in a single patient, together with human herpesvirus-6
(HHV-6) reactivation, to produce a severe, multi-organ reaction. (This case is fictional, used to
illustrate the case-report workflow.)

### Patient concerns and key clinical findings

A man in his 50s with gout, hypertension and chronic kidney disease developed fever, a widespread
maculopapular rash, facial oedema and tender lymphadenopathy beginning on the 25th day of
allopurinol treatment started for an acute gout flare, without renal dose adjustment. Laboratory
findings included eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytosis, raised transaminases and worsened renal
function.

### Diagnoses, interventions and outcomes

A RegiSCAR score of 7 supported a diagnosis of definite DRESS, with positive HLA-B*58:01
genotyping and HHV-6 reactivation on blood PCR. Allopurinol and colchicine were withdrawn and the
patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone followed by a tapering course of oral
prednisolone. Fever and rash resolved, and liver and kidney function returned close to baseline by
follow-up.

### Conclusion

In patients with chronic kidney disease, allopurinol should be started at a renally adjusted dose
and, where feasible, after HLA-B*58:01 screening; clinicians should keep DRESS in mind for fever and
rash appearing weeks, not days, after starting a new drug.

## Introduction

Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), also called drug-induced
hypersensitivity syndrome, is a severe, delayed (typically 2–8 weeks) hypersensitivity reaction
characterised by fever, a widespread rash, haematological abnormalities and internal organ
involvement; the RegiSCAR scoring system is used to classify a case as no, possible, probable or
definite DRESS [1]. Allopurinol is among the most frequently implicated drugs
[1]. Two factors are known to raise the risk of allopurinol-induced hypersensitivity:
a starting dose that is not reduced for renal function [2,4], and carriage of
the HLA-B*58:01 allele, which was found to be strongly associated with allopurinol-induced DRESS and
Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis in a Portuguese population [3].
Reactivation of human herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6) during the acute phase has also been reported in
allopurinol-induced hypersensitivity syndrome and is thought to contribute to its relapsing,
multi-organ course [5].

This case is reported (as a fictional, illustrative example) because it brings these three features
together in one patient: chronic kidney disease with a non-renally-adjusted allopurinol dose,
confirmed HLA-B*58:01 positivity, and documented HHV-6 reactivation, with sequential organ
involvement (skin, liver and kidney) that resolved after drug withdrawal and corticosteroid
treatment. It is intended to illustrate the diagnostic reasoning and management of allopurinol-DRESS
in a patient with pre-existing renal impairment, a population in whom the reaction carries
particular risk [4].

## Patient information

A man in his 50s presented with fever and a widespread rash. He had gout, with flares of the first
metatarsophalangeal joint several times a year, self-treated with colchicine during flares; and
hypertension treated with amlodipine 5 mg/day, which had been stable. He had chronic kidney disease,
with a pre-illness baseline creatinine of 118 µmol/L and an estimated glomerular filtration
rate of 58 mL/min/1.73<!-- not-a-result: standard eGFR unit denominator, not a measured value --> m². He had no known drug allergy before this episode, did not smoke
and drank alcohol lightly, and continued his usual work and daily activities before this episode.
Family history was not recorded. His HLA-B*58:01 genotype was unknown at that time, since this test
had not been performed before starting the drug that is discussed below.

His main concern at the outpatient visit before this episode was a painful gout flare. Serum uric
acid was 612 µmol/L, and allopurinol 300 mg/day was started for long-term urate
lowering, together with prophylactic colchicine; the allopurinol dose was not reduced for his known
chronic kidney disease. No HLA-B*58:01 testing was performed before starting the drug.

## Clinical findings

At admission, temperature was 39.2 °C and heart rate 104 beats/min; blood pressure
was within his usual range. He had facial oedema and a confluent erythematous maculopapular rash
involving approximately 60% of his body surface area, without blistering or mucosal
erosion, and a negative Nikolsky sign. He had bilaterally enlarged, mildly tender cervical and
inguinal lymph nodes, and a mildly enlarged, non-tender liver without jaundice.

## Diagnostic assessment

### Diagnostic methods

On day 0, full blood count showed a white-cell count of 14.6 ×10⁹/L, an eosinophil
count of 2.9 ×10⁹/L and 8% atypical lymphocytes; by day +4 these had risen to
16.2 ×10⁹/L, 3.4 ×10⁹/L and 6% respectively. On day 0, liver
biochemistry showed ALT 312 U/L, AST 241 U/L and total bilirubin 18
µmol/L; by day +4, the peak values were ALT 486 U/L, AST 355 U/L and total
bilirubin 22 µmol/L. Renal function worsened from a pre-illness creatinine of
118 µmol/L to 196 µmol/L on day 0 and a peak of 214 µmol/L on day +4.
C-reactive protein was 86 mg/L on day 0 and 64 mg/L on day +4. Blood
cultures, HBsAg, anti-HCV,
HIV serology and antinuclear antibody were all negative. HHV-6 PCR on blood was positive, at
4200 copies/mL. HLA-B*58:01 genotyping was positive. Abdominal ultrasound showed mild
hepatomegaly with a homogeneous parenchyma and normal kidneys. Skin biopsy showed a perivascular
dermatitis with a lymphocytic and eosinophilic infiltrate, without full-thickness epidermal
necrosis.

### Diagnostic challenges

The onset more than three weeks after starting allopurinol, rather than within the first days, made
an ordinary drug rash or an infectious exanthem the initial working diagnoses; the facial oedema,
lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia and multi-organ involvement were the findings that redirected the
work-up toward a severe delayed drug hypersensitivity reaction.

### Diagnostic reasoning

Fever, a widespread maculopapular rash beginning weeks after a new drug, facial oedema,
lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia with atypical lymphocytes, and combined hepatic and renal involvement
met RegiSCAR criteria for definite DRESS, with a RegiSCAR score of 7 [1].
Differential diagnoses considered and their reasons for exclusion were: Stevens-Johnson
syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (no blistering, no mucosal involvement, a negative Nikolsky
sign, and a biopsy without full-thickness epidermal necrosis); acute generalised exanthematous
pustulosis (no pustules, and a slower onset than typical); a viral exanthem or mononucleosis-like
illness (negative viral and autoimmune serologies apart from the HHV-6 reactivation, which is itself
a recognised feature of DRESS rather than an alternative diagnosis [5]); and a spontaneous
flare of chronic kidney disease unrelated to drug exposure (argued against by the clear temporal
relationship to allopurinol and by the skin and haematological findings). The positive HLA-B*58:01
result supported allopurinol specifically as the culprit drug [3].

### Prognostic characteristics

A RegiSCAR score of 7 indicated definite DRESS. HLA-B*58:01 carriage and a
non-renally-adjusted starting dose are established risk factors for developing allopurinol
hypersensitivity syndrome in the first place [2,4], and their presence here,
together with the pre-existing chronic kidney disease that could itself be worsened by the
reaction, was taken as a marker of a patient who could plausibly have a severe or protracted
course; this guided the decision for prompt systemic corticosteroid treatment and close monitoring
of renal and hepatic function.

## Therapeutic intervention

### Type of intervention

Pharmacological: withdrawal of the suspected culprit and co-prescribed drugs, systemic
corticosteroids, and supportive care.

### Dosage, strength and duration

Allopurinol (already self-discontinued by the patient) and colchicine were stopped. Intravenous
methylprednisolone was given at 70 mg/day (equivalent to 1 mg/kg/day for this
patient's body weight of 70 kg) from day 0 to day +6, followed by oral prednisolone
60 mg/day, tapered over 8 weeks. Antihistamines, a topical corticosteroid
cream and intravenous fluid support were also given. Nephrology was consulted; dialysis was not
required.

### Changes to the intervention

No dose escalation or change in the choice of immunosuppressant was needed; the oral prednisolone
dose was reduced progressively, as tolerated, over the taper period described above, guided by
resolution of the rash and by liver and kidney function.

## Follow-up and outcomes

### Outcomes

From the clinician's perspective, fever, rash, and the haematological, hepatic and renal
abnormalities all resolved. On day +18 (discharge), white-cell count was 9.8 ×10⁹/L, eosinophils 0.6 ×10⁹/L, ALT 98 U/L, AST 61 U/L, total bilirubin 14 µmol/L, creatinine 142 µmol/L and C-reactive protein 9 mg/L. <!-- cons-ok: discharge values, a different timepoint from the day 0 and day +4 values in 06-diagnostic-assessment.md -->
On day +43 (outpatient follow-up), white-cell count was 7.4 ×10⁹/L, eosinophils 0.3 ×10⁹/L and creatinine 124 µmol/L, close to his pre-illness baseline of 118 µmol/L. <!-- cons-ok: follow-up values, a different timepoint from the day 0 and day +4 values in 06-diagnostic-assessment.md -->
From the patient's perspective, his own account is given in the next section.

### Important follow-up test results

Liver enzymes and eosinophil count normalised by outpatient follow-up, and renal function returned
close to its pre-illness baseline (see the values above). A later follow-up visit, after the
corticosteroid taper was completed, confirmed no relapse of rash and normal thyroid function.

### Adherence and tolerability

Adherence and tolerability were assessed by clinical interview at the day +43 and day +62
outpatient follow-up visits. The oral prednisolone taper was completed as prescribed, with no
missed doses and no reported difficulty tolerating the corticosteroid course.

### Adverse and unanticipated events

No adverse events beyond the index drug reaction and its direct complications (transient liver and
kidney impairment) were reported during follow-up. Allopurinol was not reintroduced, and the
culprit drug was recorded in the patient's allergy record; his gout has since been managed with
lifestyle measures and colchicine for flares, with rheumatology to consider an alternative
urate-lowering drug after allergy consultation.

## Discussion

### Strengths and limitations of the management

The management followed the usual principles for DRESS: prompt withdrawal of the suspected culprit
drug, systemic corticosteroids, and close monitoring of the organs most often affected (skin, liver
and kidney) [1]. A strength of this case is that the diagnosis was supported by
objective markers beyond the clinical picture alone: a definite RegiSCAR score, confirmed
HLA-B*58:01 carriage, and documented HHV-6 reactivation. A limitation is that the allopurinol
starting dose was not adjusted for this patient's known chronic kidney disease and that HLA-B*58:01
was not checked before treatment began; both are recognised, modifiable risk factors for this
reaction [2–4], and their absence here is itself part of the
teaching point rather than a strength of the care given. Causality was assessed clinically (temporal
relationship, dechallenge, exclusion of differentials) rather than with a formal drug-causality
instrument, and the published cases chosen for comparison in Table 2 are a convenience sample of
case reports, which are more likely to be published when the course is unusual or severe
(publication bias); they illustrate that this combination of findings is possible, not how common
it is.

### Relevant literature and similar published cases

Allopurinol-induced DRESS in patients with pre-existing renal impairment, or presenting with
dominant renal involvement, has been described in several published reports, summarised in Table 2.

Across these reports and the present case, allopurinol-DRESS with renal involvement occurred weeks
(not days) after starting the drug, and corticosteroids combined with drug withdrawal led to
recovery in most patients, although one case progressed to haemodialysis-requiring renal failure
despite treatment [7]. HHV-6 reactivation was documented in both that case and the present
one, consistent with reports that herpesvirus reactivation can accompany, and may contribute to, the
multi-organ course of allopurinol hypersensitivity [5].

### Rationale for the conclusions drawn

A single case cannot establish how often HLA-B*58:01 carriage, non-renally-adjusted dosing or HHV-6
reactivation independently drive severity; the combination of risk factors here shows that such a
severe course is possible, not how frequent it is. The temporal relationship to allopurinol, the
RegiSCAR score, the resolution after drug withdrawal and corticosteroids, and the exclusion of the
main differential diagnoses together support allopurinol, rather than another exposure, as the
cause in this patient.

### Take-away lessons

In a patient with chronic kidney disease who needs a urate-lowering drug, the allopurinol starting
dose should be adjusted for renal function, and HLA-B*58:01 testing considered where available,
before treatment begins [2–4]; fever and rash appearing weeks
after starting allopurinol should prompt early consideration of DRESS, with prompt drug withdrawal
and systemic corticosteroids, and close monitoring of liver and kidney function even after clinical
improvement begins [1,7].
